The three statements, and how they lock together

Module 01. About five hours. Nothing here assumes you have seen an annual report before.

We are going to open a bakery. Not read about one — open one, put money in, buy an oven, sell cakes, and watch what happens to the numbers. By the end you will have built all three financial statements yourself, and every word that appears in a real annual report will have arrived because you needed it, not because somebody defined it at you.

Take this slowly. If a paragraph does not land, stop and read it again before going on, because everything after it leans on it.

Part one

Two lists, and nothing else

Forget accounting for a moment. Suppose you wanted to describe a business to somebody in the simplest possible way. You could write two lists.

The first list: what the business has. Money in the bank. An oven. Bags of flour. A van.

The second list: who has a claim on those things. The bank that lent money. The supplier waiting to be paid. And you, the owner.

That is a balance sheet. There is no third list and no hidden cleverness. The whole document is those two lists, written down on one particular day.

Part two

Now the words

You have just built a balance sheet without using a single technical term. Here are the names for what you built. Each is only a label for something you have already watched happen.

Asset

Anything the business has that is expected to be useful to it. Cash, the oven, the flour, and the ₹1,20,000 the hotel owes you. Everything in your left column.

Notice that money owed to you is an asset. It is not cash, but it is something the business has.

Liability

Anything the business owes to somebody who is not an owner. The bank loan, and the ₹1,00,000 owed to the ingredient supplier.

The word simply means an obligation. If you shut the bakery tomorrow, these people would still have to be paid.

Equity

The owner's claim. In the bakery it was two things: the ₹10,00,000 you put in, and the ₹1,25,000 of profit the business earned and kept.

Also called shareholders' equity, net worth, or book value. In a listed company the owners are the shareholders, so equity is the shareholders' claim.

Part three

Why the two sides always match

The totals stayed equal at every single step, including the ones where nothing obvious connected the two sides. That was not a coincidence, and it is not a rule somebody invented. It is true for a reason worth understanding properly.

Every single thing the business has was paid for by somebody. There is no other way it could be there. Either a lender's money bought it, or a supplier's goods became it, or the owner's money bought it, or the business earned it — and what the business earns belongs to the owner. So for every rupee of stuff on the left, there is a rupee of somebody's claim on the right. Necessarily.

Written down:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

Rearranged, it says something more useful:

Equity = Assets − Liabilities

The owner gets whatever is left after everybody else's claim is settled. That is why equity is called the residual claim — residual meaning leftover. This is not a small technical detail. It explains the entire risk of owning shares.

Why owners come last

The bank has a contract. The supplier has an invoice. Both can take you to court. You, as owner, have no such contract with your own business — you simply receive what remains. If the bakery does badly, the bank and supplier are still paid in full and your ₹10,00,000 absorbs the damage. If it does well, everybody else still gets only what they were promised, and the whole of the upside is yours.

That is exactly what you buy when you buy a share. Last in the queue, unlimited upside.

Part four

What a photograph cannot tell you

The balance sheet describes one day. Take one the day you opened and another a year later, and you can see equity went from ₹10,00,000 to ₹11,25,000.

So you made ₹1,25,000. But you have no idea how. Did you sell a great many cakes at a thin margin, or very few at a fat one? Did rent double? Did you win one enormous wedding order and sell almost nothing else all year? A photograph taken on 31 March cannot answer any of that, because it records nothing about what happened in between.

That is the job of the second statement.

Part five

The profit statement is a queue of people getting paid

The profit and loss statement — also called the P&L, the income statement, or in Indian annual reports the statement of profit and loss — covers a stretch of time. Usually a full year, sometimes a quarter.

Here is the picture that makes it click. Money comes in from customers at the top. Then a queue of people take their cut, in a strict order, and whatever survives the queue belongs to the owners.

The order is not arbitrary. It is the order of who can force you to pay them.

Now the names, which are simply the resting points between claimants.

Revenue

What customers were charged for goods or services during the year. The top of the queue. In Indian reports it appears as revenue from operations.

Also called sales, turnover, or the top line — because it is literally the top line of the statement.

EBITDA

What is left after paying everyone needed to keep the doors open day to day: ingredient suppliers, staff, landlord, electricity board.

The letters stand for Earnings Before Interest, Tax, Depreciation and Amortisation. The name literally lists what has not yet been subtracted. It measures how well the operation itself runs, ignoring how it was funded.

EBIT

EBITDA after subtracting the wearing out of equipment. Earnings Before Interest and Tax.

Also called operating profit. Closer to the truth than EBITDA, because ovens really do wear out and somebody eventually has to buy a new one.

PBT

Profit Before Tax. EBIT after paying interest to lenders.

The gap between EBIT and PBT is exactly what the company's borrowing costs it each year.

PAT

Profit After Tax. What is left for the owners once the government has taken its share.

Also called net profit, the bottom line, or simply "profit". The most quoted number in any annual report and, on its own, one of the least informative.

Why bother with the middle stops

Picture two bakeries with identical shops, identical staff and identical sales. One was built with the owner's money, the other with a large bank loan. Their EBITDA will be the same. Their PAT will be very different, because one pays interest and the other does not.

So if you want to judge the bakery as a business, EBITDA compares them fairly. If you want to know what you would earn as its owner, PAT is the number. They answer different questions, which is why both exist.

Part six

Depreciation, taken slowly

This one confuses nearly everybody at first, so we will go carefully.

You paid ₹4,00,000 for the oven. That money left the bank on one day. But the oven is not used up on that day — it will bake cakes for roughly ten years.

So here is the question. Does the whole ₹4,00,000 count as a cost of year one? If it did, year one would look terrible and years two through ten would look wonderful, and neither picture would describe the bakery honestly. Year seven benefits from that oven just as much as year one did.

Accounting's answer is to spread the cost across the years the oven is actually used. Ten years, so ₹40,000 charged as a cost each year. That yearly charge is depreciation.

Depreciation

The portion of a long-lived asset's cost charged as an expense this year, reflecting that the asset has been partly used up.

No money moves when depreciation is charged. The money moved years earlier, when the asset was bought. Amortisation is the identical idea applied to things you cannot touch, like software or a patent.

Hold on to this, because it returns shortly: depreciation reduces profit but takes no cash. If you are working out how much money a business actually generated, you will have to undo it.

One more term belongs here, because it follows directly.

To capitalise, versus to expense

When a company spends money it must decide whether the spending benefits only this year or many years to come. If only this year — flour, salaries, electricity — the whole amount hits the profit statement immediately. That is expensing it.

If it benefits many years — an oven, a building — the amount goes onto the balance sheet as an asset and reaches the profit statement slowly, as depreciation. That is capitalising it.

Both spend the same cash. But capitalising makes this year's profit look higher. Companies sometimes capitalise things that should have been expensed for precisely that reason, which makes this a place worth watching.

Part seven

The gap between profit and money

Go back to the last step of the bakery. You delivered ₹1,20,000 of cakes to a hotel and your profit rose by ₹75,000. Your bank balance did not move by a single rupee.

Was it right to record that profit? Yes. You baked the cakes, you delivered them, the hotel has them and owes you. The work is done. Waiting for payment before recording anything would make your March look disastrous and your June look miraculous, when in truth March is when the business actually performed.

This principle has a name.

Accrual

Recording revenue when it is earned and costs when they are incurred, regardless of when cash moves.

Every listed company's profit statement is prepared this way. It is the reason profit and cash are two different things rather than two words for one thing.

Trade receivables

Money customers owe the business for goods already delivered. The ₹1,20,000 from the hotel.

Often shortened to receivables, or called debtors in older reports. It sits on the balance sheet as an asset until the customer pays, at which point it turns into cash.

Now the uncomfortable part. Suppose the hotel never pays. Or suppose a company chases growth by selling to customers it knows are slow, or shaky, or likely to return the goods next quarter. Revenue rises. Profit rises. Cash never arrives. And the profit statement, prepared honestly under the rules, shows a wonderful year.

That is why a third statement exists.

Part eight

Three buckets, and the question that sorts them

The cash flow statement ignores accrual entirely and tracks one thing only: rupees actually entering and leaving the bank account.

Every movement gets sorted into one of three buckets. The sorting question is simply: what was the business doing when this money moved?

Try it on the case that trips people up. Where does a dividend go? A dividend is money handed back to the owners — the people who funded the business. Nobody is baking anything, and nothing long-lived is being bought. It is funding, flowing in reverse. So: financing. You did not memorise that, you worked it out.

Try another. Buying a delivery van? Long-lived, bought to build capacity. Investing.

CFO — cash from operating activities

The cash a business generated by simply doing its job, before spending anything on expansion and before any dealings with lenders or owners.

If you only ever look at one number in a cash flow statement, look at this one.

Why does CFO matter so much more than the other two? Because the other two can look healthy for years while the business is failing. Investing turns positive when a company sells off its own assets. Financing turns positive when a company borrows. Neither is a business earning money. Only CFO is.

Why depreciation gets added back

Cash flow statements usually start at profit before tax and work backwards to cash. The first adjustment is almost always add back depreciation, which looks like cheating until you remember Part six.

Depreciation was subtracted to arrive at profit. Correctly — the oven really is wearing out. But no cash left when it was charged. So if you are converting profit into cash, you must put back anything that reduced profit without touching the bank account. That is all the add-back is: undoing a non-cash deduction.

Why changes in stock and receivables show up

The same logic, running the other way. Profit counted the ₹1,20,000 hotel sale, but the cash never came. So to get from profit to cash you subtract the rise in receivables. Money you earned but have not collected is money you do not have.

The reverse holds too. When the supplier lets you owe them ₹1,00,000, you received goods without paying. That is cash you are still holding, so it gets added.

Working capital

The short-term items that swing back and forth as a business trades: stock, money owed by customers, money owed to suppliers.

When a business grows it usually has to carry more stock and wait on more customer payments. That ties up cash. It is why fast-growing companies are so often short of money despite being profitable.

Part nine

Three more words you will meet constantly

Reserves, or retained earnings

Profit the business earned in the past and did not hand to its owners. It sits inside equity.

In the bakery this was the "profit you have earned" line. In an annual report it appears as other equity or reserves and surplus. Each year it grows by that year's profit and shrinks by any dividend paid.

Standalone versus consolidated

A large company often owns other companies. Standalone shows only the parent company by itself. Consolidated shows the parent plus everything it controls, added together as one economic unit.

If a company's real operations sit in its subsidiaries, standalone figures show you an almost empty shell. Read consolidated by default.

Capital expenditure, or capex

Money spent buying long-lived assets. Your ₹4,00,000 oven was capex.

It appears in the investing bucket of the cash flow statement, not in the profit statement — where it shows up only slowly, as depreciation.

Part eleven

The four links

The three statements are not three documents that happen to be printed together. They are three views of one system, and each closes a loop in the others. If you can trace these four links, you understand how a set of accounts holds together.

  • Profit becomes reserves. Reserves opened at 220, profit was 120, reserves closed at 340. It ties exactly — which tells you no dividend was paid, because a dividend would have taken some of that 120 away before it reached reserves.
  • Profit starts the cash flow. The cash flow statement begins at profit before tax, 160, and adjusts from there.
  • Working capital adjusts it. Stock rose 70 and receivables rose 90, so that much profit never became cash. Payables and other liabilities rose 110, so that much cash was held on to.
  • Closing cash returns to the balance sheet. Cash opened at 60, fell 15 across the year, and the balance sheet shows 45.

One more is worth deriving yourself. Fixed assets opened at 480. Depreciation of 70 reduced them. They closed at 635. So the company must have bought 635 − 480 + 70 = 225 of new assets. That is exactly the capex figure in the cash flow. Companies do not always label capex helpfully, and this is how you find it.

Part twelve

What these statements are telling you

Now the point of everything above. Two things here would make an experienced analyst stop, and neither is visible in the profit figure.

Cash fell in a year of record profit. Profit was 120, yet cash dropped 15. The lazy reading is that the profit is fake. The correct reading is more interesting. Operations threw off 180 — more than profit, which is healthy. The entire shortfall came from spending 225 on new outlets, funded partly by borrowing 70. This is a company investing faster than it earns. That might be excellent or reckless, depending entirely on whether the new outlets earn their keep, which one year of statements cannot tell you. But you now know precisely what question to ask.

Customers are taking much longer to pay. Revenue grew 33%. Receivables grew 90%. Turn that into days and customers took about 41 days to pay last year and about 58 days this year.

Something changed. Perhaps the company shifted towards large institutional buyers who always pay slowly, which is fine. Perhaps it loosened credit terms to hit a growth target, which is less fine. Perhaps some of those receivables will never be collected at all, which is not fine. Three very different stories — and one number surfaced all of them.

The habit to build

Never read profit on its own. Always read it next to cash from operations. When the two drift apart for three years or more, you have found either an accounting problem or a business whose economics you do not yet understand. Both are worth your time.

Where to find all this for a real company

  • The company's own investor relations page, for the full annual report.
  • BSE and NSE corporate filings, for quarterly results as they are filed.
  • Screener.in, for a fast ten-year view. Good for a first look, but it is a processed dataset — go to the annual report before you form a real opinion.

Before Module 02, do one thing. Open the latest annual report of any company you follow. Find the three statements. Then check a single number: did reserves grow by exactly the year's profit, less any dividend paid? If it ties, you have just read a set of accounts. If it does not, find out why — the answer is always in the notes, and it is always worth knowing.

Part A — did it land

01You bought the oven for ₹4,00,000 in cash. Immediately afterwards, the total of everything the business owned:

c. You swapped one asset for another. ₹4,00,000 of cash left and ₹4,00,000 of oven arrived. Nobody new gained a claim on the business, so the right-hand column did not move either.

02The supplier delivers ₹1,00,000 of ingredients and gives you 30 days to pay. On the day of delivery:

a. You received goods, so stock rises on the left. You gave a promise to pay, so an obligation rises on the right. Both sides move by ₹1,00,000 and stay equal. Option d is the instinct to unlearn — a great deal happens without cash moving.

03Shareholders' equity is best described as:

c. Equity is assets minus liabilities — the residual. It includes money put in and profits retained, so b is incomplete. And it is a book figure built from the accounts, which is why it usually differs from what the shares trade at.

04Depreciation of ₹40,000 is charged on the oven this year. The cash leaving the bank because of this charge is:

c. The entire ₹4,00,000 left the bank in year one, when the oven was bought. Depreciation is a bookkeeping charge spreading that historical cost across the years of use. No further cash moves, ever.

05You deliver ₹1,20,000 of cakes to the hotel, which will pay in 60 days. On the day of delivery:

a. Accrual: revenue is recorded when earned, and you earned it by delivering. The ₹1,20,000 sits on the balance sheet as a trade receivable until the money actually arrives.

Part B — the working level

06Vardhan reported profit of ₹120 lakh, yet its cash balance fell by ₹15 lakh. The best explanation is:

b. Operations were strongly cash-positive at ₹180 lakh — more than profit. The drain was investing. Depreciation is non-cash and cannot drain anything. And the interest added back in operating is the same interest shown in financing: a reclassification, not a double count.

07Receivable days moved from roughly 41 to roughly 58 while revenue grew 33%. The most defensible reading is:

c. Rising receivable days means money is being recognised as revenue further ahead of collection. It is not proof of anything improper — a shift towards institutional customers does this legitimately — but it demands an explanation from the notes.

08A company capitalises ₹50 lakh of spending that its competitors expense. In year one, compared with expensing it, this:

a. The ₹50 lakh leaves the bank either way, so closing cash is identical. What changes is where it appears. Capitalising moves the outflow from operating to investing, which flatters CFO, and parks the cost on the balance sheet so it reaches profit only slowly as depreciation. This is one of the commonest ways a business is made to look better than it is.

09Vardhan's reserves rose from ₹220 lakh to ₹340 lakh — exactly the year's profit. This tells you:

c. Reserves close at opening plus profit, less dividends and anything else appropriated. A perfect tie means nothing was taken out. Had shares been issued above face value, the excess would also have landed in reserves and broken the tie.

10Which of these leaves total assets unchanged?

c. Collection turns a receivable into cash — value moves within the left column and the total is unchanged. This is the oven purchase from Part one wearing a different costume. The other three all expand both sides at once.

11A dividend paid to shareholders appears in the cash flow statement under:

c. Ask the sorting question: what was the business doing? Not baking, and not buying anything long-lived. It was moving money between itself and the people who fund it. Financing covers that traffic in both directions — loans in and out, shares issued and bought back, dividends paid.

12A holding company earns most of its profit through operating subsidiaries. To judge the business you should work primarily from:

b. Standalone shows the parent alone, where a holding company's operations barely appear — you would be reading a nearly empty shell. Consolidation eliminates transactions within the group rather than double counting them. Standalone is still worth a look, but as a comparison that reveals how much value sits outside the parent.

13A company shows rising profit for three consecutive years while cash from operations is negative every year, with the gap funded by fresh borrowing. The most defensible reading is:

d. Notice how carefully d is worded. You have not established fraud, and b explains nothing — understating depreciation would raise profit without touching CFO at all, since depreciation is added straight back. What you can say is that three years of divergence, plugged with borrowing, is not sustainable and demands an explanation. That restraint is the shape of a real research conclusion: state exactly what the evidence supports, and stop there.
