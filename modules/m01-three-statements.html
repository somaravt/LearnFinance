The three statements, and how they lock together

Module 01. About five hours. Read, work the example, then sit the test.

Almost everything else in equity research sits on top of this module. Valuation, ratios, red flags, sector work — all of it reads from three statements. Get these wrong and every later conclusion inherits the error.

Why a company publishes anything at all

A listed company has taken money from strangers. The annual report is the price of that money: a periodic, audited account of what was done with it. That framing matters, because it tells you what the document is for and what it is not. It is a defence of stewardship, prepared by the people being judged. It is accurate in a narrow legal sense and selective in a broad practical sense.

Your job as an analyst is to read a document written by an interested party and extract from it a view the writer did not intend to hand you. That is a skill, and it starts with knowing what each statement is structurally capable of hiding.

The balance sheet is a photograph

Taken on one specific day, usually 31 March for an Indian company. It answers one question: on that date, what did the business own, and who has a claim on it?

Everything the company controls sits on one side. Every claim against those things sits on the other. Since every rupee of resource came from somewhere, the two sides are equal by construction:

Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders' equity

This is not a discovery, it is a definition. The balance sheet balances the way a ledger balances — because it was built to. What is informative is not that it balances but how: which assets the business is carrying, and whose money is funding them.

Shareholders' equity is the residual. It is what would be left for owners if every asset fetched exactly its carrying value and every liability were settled. Both of those assumptions are usually wrong, which is why book value and market value diverge.

The profit and loss statement is a film

Where the balance sheet is one day, the P&L covers a stretch of time — a quarter or a year. It records what the business earned and what it consumed in that window.

Read it as a descent. Revenue enters at the top, and successive categories of cost are removed until what remains belongs to shareholders. The intermediate stopping points each answer a different question:

  • EBITDA — what the operations produced before accounting for the cost of the assets used, the cost of debt, and tax. Useful for comparing operating performance across companies with different debt loads. Dangerous when treated as cash.
  • EBIT — after depreciation, so it now reflects that machines wear out. This is closer to the true economic output of the operations.
  • PBT — after interest. The gap between EBIT and PBT is the price of the company's debt.
  • PAT — after tax. The bottom line, and the most quoted and least sufficient number in the whole report.

The cash flow statement is the check on both

Profit is an opinion; cash is a fact. That line is a cliché because it is true, and this statement is where the opinion gets tested.

A sale becomes revenue in the P&L when it is earned, not when the customer pays. That is the accrual principle, and it is a reasonable convention — otherwise a company that delivers in March and collects in April would look catastrophic in one year and heroic in the next. But it opens a gap. A company can report rising profit for years while collecting nothing, and only the cash flow statement will say so.

It has three sections:

  • Operating (CFO) — cash actually thrown off by running the business. The single most important number in the report.
  • Investing (CFI) — cash spent buying assets or received selling them. Usually negative for a growing company, and that is healthy.
  • Financing (CFF) — cash raised from or returned to lenders and shareholders. Borrowings, repayments, dividends, buybacks.
The reconciliation that trips everyone up

CFO usually starts at profit before tax and then adds back depreciation. This looks like cheating. It is not. Depreciation was subtracted in the P&L, but no rupee left the building when it was — the cash left years ago, when the asset was bought, and it appeared in investing that year. Adding it back simply undoes a non-cash deduction.

How the three lock together

This is the part worth slowing down on. The statements are not three separate documents that happen to be bound together; they are three views of one system, and each one closes a loop in the others.

Trace each one in Vardhan's numbers and you will not forget them:

  • Reserves. Opening 220, plus PAT of 120, closes at 340. It ties exactly, which tells you something: the company paid no dividend this year. If reserves had risen by less than PAT, the difference would be a dividend or some other appropriation.
  • Fixed assets. Opening 480, less depreciation of 70, plus capex of 225, closes at 635. Capex is not usually disclosed as a single friendly line — you often have to derive it exactly this way.
  • Cash. Opening 60, less the net outflow of 15, closes at 45, which is the figure on the balance sheet.

What Vardhan is actually telling you

Now the point of all this. Two things in these statements would make an experienced analyst stop and dig, and neither is visible in the headline profit number.

Cash fell in a year of record profit. PAT was ₹120 lakh, yet the cash balance dropped ₹15 lakh. The naive reading is that the profit is fake. The correct reading is more interesting: operations generated a healthy ₹180 lakh, more than profit, and the entire shortfall came from spending ₹225 lakh on new outlets. That is a company investing ahead of its cash generation and funding the gap with debt. Whether that is aggressive growth or overreach depends on whether the new outlets earn their keep — which you cannot tell from one year of statements. But you now know exactly what question to ask.

Receivables grew far faster than sales. Revenue rose 33%. Receivables rose 90%. Converted to days, customers took about 41 days to pay in FY25 and about 58 days in FY26. Something changed: the company is selling to slower-paying institutional buyers, or it loosened credit terms to hit a growth number, or some of those receivables will never be collected. Three very different stories, one number that surfaced all of them.

The habit to build

Never read PAT alone. Read PAT next to CFO, every time. When the two diverge persistently over three or more years, you have either found an accounting problem or a business whose economics you do not yet understand. Both are worth your time.

Where to get these for a real company

  • The company's own investor relations page, for the full annual report including notes and the auditor's report.
  • BSE and NSE corporate filings, for quarterly results and disclosures as they are filed.
  • Screener.in, for a fast standardised ten-year view. Excellent for triage, but it is a derived dataset — go to the annual report before you form a real view.

Before the next module, do one thing: open the latest annual report of any company you already own or follow, find these three statements, and check that reserves moved by PAT less dividend. If it does not tie, find out why. That single exercise is worth more than re-reading this page.

Mock test

Ten questions. Attempt every one, including the ones you are unsure of — guessing forces you to commit to a reading, which is the skill being trained.

01Vardhan reported PAT of ₹120 lakh, yet its cash balance fell by ₹15 lakh. The best explanation is:

b. Operations were strongly cash-positive at ₹180 lakh, more than PAT. The drain was investing. Depreciation is non-cash and cannot drain anything, and the finance cost added back in operations is the same one paid in financing — a reclassification, not a double count.

02Receivable days moved from roughly 41 to roughly 58 while revenue grew 33%. The most defensible reading is:

c. Rising receivable days means money is being recognised as revenue further ahead of actual collection. It is not proof of wrongdoing — a shift toward institutional customers does this legitimately — but it is exactly the kind of divergence that demands an explanation from the notes.

03Depreciation is added back when deriving cash from operations because:

b. The cash left when the asset was purchased, and appeared in investing that year. Depreciation spreads that historical cost across the P&L over time. Option c is a common and expensive misconception — depreciation sets aside nothing.

04A company capitalises ₹50 lakh of spending that its competitors expense. Compared with expensing it, in year one this:

a. The ₹50 lakh still leaves the bank either way, so the closing cash balance is identical. What changes is classification: capitalising moves the outflow from operating to investing, which flatters CFO, and parks the cost on the balance sheet so it only hits profit slowly through depreciation. This is one of the most common ways a business is made to look better than it is.

05Vardhan's other equity rose from ₹220 lakh to ₹340 lakh — exactly the year's PAT. This tells you:

c. Reserves close at opening plus profit, less dividends and any other appropriations. A perfect tie means nothing was taken out. Had shares been issued at a premium, the premium would also have landed in other equity and broken the tie.

06Which of these transactions leaves total assets unchanged?

c. Collection converts a receivable into cash — value moves within the asset side and the total is unchanged. The other three all expand both sides of the balance sheet simultaneously, which is why the equation survives each one.

07Dividend paid by a company appears in the cash flow statement under:

c. Financing covers every movement between the company and its capital providers, in both directions — borrowings and repayments, share issues and buybacks, dividends. A dividend is capital returned to owners.

08A company reports total assets of ₹1,035 lakh and total liabilities of ₹595 lakh. Shareholders' equity is:

b. Equity is the residual: 1,035 − 595 = 440. You never need the P&L to compute it, because the balance sheet balances by construction. This is Vardhan's FY26 equity.

09A holding company earns most of its profit through operating subsidiaries. To judge the business you should work primarily from:

b. Standalone shows only the parent entity, where a holding company's operations barely appear — you would be reading an almost empty shell. Consolidation eliminates intra-group transactions rather than double counting them. Standalone still matters, but as a comparison that reveals how much value sits outside the parent.

10A company shows rising PAT for three consecutive years while cash from operations is negative every year, with the gap funded by fresh borrowing. The most defensible reading is:

d. Note the precision of the claim. You have not established fraud, and understated depreciation would raise profit without touching CFO, so b explains nothing. What you can say is that three years of divergence between profit and operating cash, plugged with debt, is not sustainable and demands an explanation. That is the shape of a defensible research conclusion: state what the evidence supports, and no more.

Vardhan Bakers Limited is fictional. Its figures were constructed so that all three statements tie exactly, which real statements also do — and which is the first thing worth checking in any report you read.
